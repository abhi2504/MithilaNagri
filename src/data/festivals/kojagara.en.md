On the bright full moon of **Ashwin** — the night the rest of India keeps as Sharad Purnima — Mithila celebrates **Kojagara**, and in a form found almost nowhere else: the festival of the **newly-wed groom**. The **journey above** follows the night from the bride's gift to the moonlit makhana; here is what makes it Mithila's own.

## The festival of the son-in-law
Where the pan-Indian Sharad Purnima is chiefly a Lakshmi night, Mithila turns it into a celebration of the new **jamai** (son-in-law). In the weeks after a wedding, the bride's family sends a **bhaar** — a ceremonial load of **makhana, paan, batasha, khaja**, fruit and new clothes — to the groom's home, where the women bless him with the *chumawan* on a fresh *aripan*. Tradition traces the custom to **King Janaka**, said to have first kept it for his son-in-law **Rama**, binding the festival to Mithila's founding story (some local tellings instead have Sita sending the gifts).

## Lakshmi, and the night-long watch
Kojagara is also a **Lakshmi** night — she is worshipped (often as the household Grihalakshmi) under the full moon, whose rays are believed to carry a nectar-like, health-giving power. The name itself means **"who is awake?"** (*ko jagarti*): Lakshmi is said to roam the earth that night blessing the wakeful, so families keep an all-night vigil, passing the hours at **Pachisi** with cowries and dice. **Makhana kheer** is left in the moonlight and fed to the new groom for health and long life, and the makhana, paan and batasha are then shared across the neighbourhood — an act read as a sign of Mithila's social harmony.

## Today
Kojagara is still widely kept across Mithilanchal, the *bhaar* and the moonlit makhana intact, and is cherished as one of the festivals most distinctively the region's own — the makhana, paan and full-moon Lakshmi all quintessentially Maithil.
