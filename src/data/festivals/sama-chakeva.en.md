In the bright fortnight after Chhath, the girls and women of Mithila keep **Sama-Chakeva**, a tender festival of the bond between sister and brother, played out with hand-moulded clay birds under the open winter sky. The **journey above** follows it from the moulding to the farewell; here is the story and meaning beneath it.

## A festival of birds and the season
Sama-Chakeva is a women's folk festival of the Mithila plains — Bihar's Maithil districts and the Madhesh of Nepal (where the Tharu community keeps it too). It is tied to a real event in nature: each **Kartik**, flocks of birds migrate south from the Himalayas onto the plains, and the painted clay birds the sisters mould are read as a welcome to these winter visitors. Its antiquity is oral rather than dated; the legend is attributed to the *Skanda Purana*.

## The legend
By the folk telling, **Sama**, daughter of Krishna, lived devoutly in a Vrindavan grove with her husband **Chakeva**. A gossip, **Chugla**, falsely accused her before Krishna, who in anger cursed her into a bird; the loyal Chakeva chose to share her fate. Her brother **Samb**, through prayer and a women's ritual, won her restoration. So the festival both celebrates the **sister–brother bond** and ritually **destroys slander** — the sisters burn the moustache of the clay Chugla each night.

## Today
Sama-Chakeva survives as one of the most distinctive markers of Maithil identity — part theatre, part prayer, part winter game. Urban participation has thinned, but the clay-figure craft and the women's songs are now also celebrated as living heritage, and the festival's gentle ecology — honouring migratory birds, returning the clay to the water at *visarjan* — is increasingly cherished.
