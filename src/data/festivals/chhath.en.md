**Chhath** is the great festival of Mithila — a four-day rite of thanksgiving to the **sun-god Surya** and the goddess **Chhathi Maiya**, kept with a discipline and a directness found in few other Hindu festivals. There is no temple, no idol and no priest: the devotee worships the sun face to face, standing in the river. The **day-by-day journey above** follows the rite from the first bath to the dawn offering; here is where it comes from, why it is kept, and what it asks of those who keep it.

## Origins & history
Sun-worship is among the oldest layers of Hindu practice — the **Rigveda** sings to Surya, and the dawn goddess **Ushas** (whom Chhathi Maiya is identified with) is Vedic. The festival's *stories*, though, are devotional rather than dated: tradition holds that **Sita** kept Chhath after returning to Ayodhya, that **Karna**, son of the Sun, worshipped standing in water, and that **Draupadi** and **Kunti** observed it for the Pandavas. Beneath the legends, Chhath reads most clearly as an ancient **post-harvest thanksgiving of the rice-growing Gangetic plain** — which is exactly when, and where, it is kept. The name comes from *shashthi*, "the sixth", the lunar day of the main offering.

## Why it is done — and the setting sun
Chhath is gratitude to the **life-giver**: Surya, visible source of energy and health, and Chhathi Maiya, protector of children, to whom families vow for **progeny, health and the family's welfare**. Its most striking feature is that the first great offering is made not to the rising but to the **setting sun** — reverence for the sun's whole arc, gratitude offered even to the light as it declines, before the dawn *arghya* completes the circle. Popular writing also praises the festival's sunrise-and-sunset exposure, fasting and riverbank gathering as "scientific" and eco-friendly; these are best read as *modern rationales* rather than the reason it began.

## What it asks — the rigour
Chhath is one of the most demanding of all Hindu vows. The *vrati* keeps a roughly **36-hour waterless (nirjala) fast** from the Kharna meal until the dawn offering; observes strict purity — sleeping on the floor, using only earthen, bamboo or brass vessels, no leather; and the *prasad*, above all the iconic **thekua**, is cooked at home on a clay stove, **satvik**, without onion, garlic or even salt. Traditionally the vrati is a married woman praying for her family, though men keep it too.

## The songs, the spread, and today
The soul of Chhath is its **folk songs** — women's voices at the ghat through the night vigil, addressing Chhathi Maiya and *Dinanath* the Sun. The defining voice is **Sharda Sinha**, the "Bihar Kokila", whose Maithili and Bhojpuri Chhath geet — *"Kelwa ke paat par"*, *"Ho Dinanath"* — are inseparable from the festival. From its homeland in Mithila and the rest of Bihar, Chhath has spread across eastern India, the **Nepal Tarai** (great gatherings at Janakpur) and the global diaspora; India has moved to nominate it for UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list (a nomination **in progress**, not yet granted). The image of thousands standing in the water at dawn and dusk is now one of the most recognisable in all of India.
