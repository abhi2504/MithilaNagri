**Madhushravani** is a festival kept by **newly-married Maithil women** in the first year of marriage, across some two weeks of the monsoon month of **Shravan** — its name joining *madhu*, "honey", to the month, the sweetness of a new marriage. The **journey above** follows the daily worship from the opening vow to the final test; here is what it means.

## Two models of the ideal wife
The festival is built around two devotional stories the bride is meant to absorb. In the first, **Gauri (Parvati)** wins Shiva as her eternal husband through penance — so the bride worships Gauri daily for her own marriage. In the second, the serpent-goddess **Bishahari (Manasa)** kills the merchant's son **Lakhindar** with a snakebite on his wedding night, and his bride **Bihula** floats on a raft with his body all the way to the gods, winning his life back by sheer devotion. Beneath the legends lies a real monsoon fear — **snakebite** — which the worship of the Nag-Nagin seeks to ward off.

## A daily, women-led rite
For the fortnight the bride lives under special discipline, guided by an elder woman, the **bidkari**. Each morning she gathers fresh flowers and leaves; each day she worships Gauri, the serpent deities and the planets; each evening the bidkari narrates a section of a long *katha* cycle. It is intimate, women's ritual — a young woman's induction into the web of Maithil custom.

## The test, and its reform
The festival once ended with the **tela-baati** (*dagdhi*): a ghee-soaked wick applied to the bride's knees, the burn a folk-omen of her husband's long life, borne without flinching. This is **historical folk belief, not health fact**, and it is the festival's most criticised element — today widely **reformed**, with families performing a harmless *sheetal-temi* (the wick only brought near, or a circle drawn in red sandal paste), a change driven largely by women themselves. Once near-forgotten, Madhushravani is now increasingly profiled as endangered Maithil heritage worth keeping.
