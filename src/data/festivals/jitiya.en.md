**Jitiya** (*Jivitputrika*) is among the most demanding of all Maithil vows — three days centred on a roughly 24-hour **nirjala** fast, without food or even water, kept by mothers for the long life of their children. The **journey above** walks through the three days; here is where it comes from and why mothers keep so hard a fast.

## Origins
Jitiya is an ancient women's *vrat* of the eastern Gangetic plain — Mithila and the rest of Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand and the Nepal Tarai. Its hero, the Vidyadhara prince **Jimutavahana**, is the subject of Emperor **Harsha's seventh-century Sanskrit play *Nagananda*** — a real textual anchor, even if the festival's link to it is traditional.

## Why it is done — two legends
Two devotional stories give the fast its meaning. In the first, the compassionate prince **Jimutavahana** offers his own body to the eagle **Garuda** in place of a doomed serpent youth; moved, Garuda spares the snakes — the archetype of selfless protection of another's child that mothers invoke for their own. In the second, the **eagle and the jackal** (*Chilho-Siyarin*) both vow to keep Jitiya, but the jackal breaks the fast; reborn as sisters, the faithful eagle's children thrive while the jackal's are lost — until devout, unbroken observance restores them. The tale is why the fast must be kept **without lapse**.

## Today
Across Bihar and the Maithil diaspora, Jitiya remains very widely kept, a point of fierce maternal pride. In Jharkhand it can stretch to eight days; the eagle-and-jackal *katha* and the *marua* (millet) and *noni saag* of the *Paran* meal remain the marks of an authentic Jitiya — and, given the genuinely waterless fast, of real endurance.
