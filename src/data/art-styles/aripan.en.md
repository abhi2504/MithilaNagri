**Aripan** (also *aripana*; from Sanskrit *alepana*, "to smear") is Mithila's sacred floor and threshold art — the drawn ground on which the divine is invited to sit. Women prepare *pithar*, a paste of ground rice and water, dip two fingers in it, and trace flowing geometric figures freehand on a floor first swept and coated with cow dung. No deity is worshipped and no life-cycle rite — *mundan*, *upanayana*, *vivah* — is begun in a Maithil home until the proper aripan is laid; the design literally functions as the *asana*, the consecrated seat of the god being summoned.

Each occasion has its own grammar of motifs. The eight-petalled lotus (*ashtadala*) belongs to Vishnu, the *shatkona* hexagram to Bhagavati, the *tulasi yantra* to the sacred basil; footprints (*pagliya*) lead Lakshmi indoors at Diwali, and fish, swastikas and unbroken-rice (*akshata*) dots carry fertility and auspiciousness. The work is generational — elder women draw the principal figures while younger hands add the final vermilion dots — which is exactly how the visual vocabulary later flowered into Madhubani painting.

Because aripan is made fresh and erased with each ritual, it remains a living everyday women's practice rather than a marketed product, though artists now also render it on paper and cloth. Its turmeric-yellow, sindoor-red and soot-black palette and its lotus-and-yantra repertoire are the seedbed from which much of Mithila's celebrated wall and paper art grew.
