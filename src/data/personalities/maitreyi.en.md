**Maitreyi** is one of the earliest named women philosophers of India, remembered in the **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad** (c. 700 BCE) as a *brahmavadini* — an "expounder of the Veda" — within the court culture of **Videha**, the kingdom of Mithila under King Janaka. She appears as the scholarly wife of the great sage **Yajnavalkya**, contrasted with his other, worldly wife Katyayani. Her dates are legendary rather than documented.

In the famous **Maitreyi–Yajnavalkya dialogue**, when her husband prepares to renounce the world and divide his property, Maitreyi refuses wealth and asks instead for the knowledge of immortality — "What should I do with that which will not make me immortal?" Her question opens one of the Upanishad's deepest meditations on **Atman** (the Self) and **Brahman**, a cornerstone of later Advaita philosophy. Commentators from Sureshvara onward treat her not as a passive listener but as a genuine challenger of Yajnavalkya's argument.

For Mithila, Maitreyi embodies the land's ancient identity as a seat of **Vedic learning and open philosophical debate** centred on Janaka's Videha — a tradition in which women could be acknowledged seekers of metaphysical truth.
