At the heart of the Maithil wedding is the **Kohbar ghar** — the nuptial chamber whose wall bears not decoration but a coded blessing, painted by the women of the house. The classic **Kohbar** image is read symbol by symbol:

- the **purain** (lotus pond) — its ringed leaves and central bloom standing for the feminine, for the bride, for *shakti*;
- the **bamboo** (*bans*) rising beside it — the groom, and the growth of his lineage;
- and around them **fish, parrots, tortoises, snakes, the sun and moon**, and the guardian **naina-jogin** — emblems of fertility, fidelity, love and protection.

## A ritual object, not an ornament
The newly-married couple is brought before this wall to worship, and through the **Chaturthi** nights the chamber is the setting for the marriage's most intimate observances. Because the union is felt to be sealed here — before the painted lotus and bamboo — Maithils say their marriage is fixed not in the stars but **on the chamber wall**.

## From wall to canvas
It was this domestic, ritual art — the kohbar and the floor-drawn *aripan* — that the wider world later "discovered" as **Madhubani painting**. The motifs now sold on paper across the globe began here, as a private prayer for union and continuance painted for one bride and groom. It crowns the wedding described in [the Maithil wedding](/en/rituals/maithil-vivah/).
