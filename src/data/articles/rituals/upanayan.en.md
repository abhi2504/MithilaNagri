The **Upanayan** (*janeu* or *yajnopavit sanskar*) is the rite that admits a boy of the Maithil Brahmin or Karna Kayastha communities to formal learning and to the sacred thread he will wear for life. In Mithila it is no single-day affair: the *Barua* (the boy) is treated as a **bridegroom of learning**, and the ceremony — Udog, Matkor, Kumaram, the tonsure, the thread, the Gayatri, the first alms — unfolds across more than a week, woven through with the women's folk songs. The **stage-by-stage journey above** walks through each day; what follows is where the rite comes from and what it means.

## Origins & history
Upanayana is one of the oldest of the Hindu life-cycle rites — the *saṃskāra* of initiation — its procedure set out in the ancient **Gṛhyasūtras** (Āśvalāyana, Pāraskara and others) more than two thousand years ago. Its heart is the idea of a **second birth**: through the rite the boy becomes *dvija*, "twice-born", born once of his parents and again of the Gayatri and his teacher. Classically the age was fixed by *varna* — around eight for a Brahmin — and the boy then left for years of *gurukul* study.

Mithila kept the rite but made it unmistakably its own. Where many regions perform a brisk one-day ceremony, the **Maithil Upanayan** borrows the whole grammar of the wedding — the bamboo *marwa*, the **Matkor** soil-rite, the women's processions and songs, the feast — so that the boy is married, as it were, to the Vedas. This elaboration reflects the region's long obsession with **learning**: in the land of Yajnavalkya, Gangesha and the Navya-Nyaya logicians, admission to study was an event worth a week of ceremony.

## Why it is done
The rite admits the boy to **Vedic study** under a teacher and to the disciplined life of *brahmacharya* — truth, restraint, service and learning. Its central gift, the **janeu** (*yajnopavita*), is a thread of three strands knotted at the *Brahma-granthi*; the three strands are read as the **three debts** every twice-born owes — to the gods (*deva-ṛṇa*), to the sages and teachers (*ṛṣi-ṛṇa*), and to the ancestors (*pitṛ-ṛṇa*). The **Gayatri Mantra**, whispered into the ear (see the journey above), is the actual moment of initiation — the verse the boy will recite daily for the rest of his life.

## Variations & today
Practice varies by community and family: Maithil Brahmins and Karna Kayasthas keep the full sequence, and the *Kumari* feeding, the *danda* and *bhiksha*, and the disposal of the cut hair in the bamboo grove (*basbitti*) are distinctively local touches. Today the rite is often **compressed** into a few days, and frequently performed only shortly before a young man's marriage rather than in childhood. It remains, however, one of the great markers of Maithil identity and a reason families still gather in large numbers — a public declaration that a new *dvija*, and ideally a new scholar, has joined the lineage.
