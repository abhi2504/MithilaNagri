**Mundan** — the ceremonial first shaving of a child's head — is among the tender rites of Maithil childhood. The **journey above** walks through the day; here is where it comes from and why it is done.

## Origins & meaning
Mundan is the **Chudakarana**, the eighth of the classical sixteen *saṃskāras* set out in the ancient Grhyasutras. The shedding of the **birth-hair** is understood as a shedding of impurities and traits carried over from past lives — a cleansing that lets the child begin afresh. A single tuft, the **shikha**, is left at the crown, the mark of a Hindu life and (for boys) the thread that connects this rite forward to the [Upanayan](/en/rituals/upanayan/).

## When and how, in Mithila
The rite is held at an **odd age** — one, three or five years (occasionally up to seven) — on a day fixed by the family priest from the Panchang. It is usually performed at the **family Kuldevi shrine, a Devi-sthan, or a riverbank**, where the deity's blessing is sought; the child sits in the lap of an elderly woman who gathers the falling hair in the end of her sari, and the cut hair is then offered to a river or laid at the roots of a bamboo. Afterwards the women perform *chumaon* with rice and durva grass, the child is bathed and newly clothed, and a **bhoj** with sweets and folk songs closes the day.

## In the arc of childhood
Mundan sits within the sequence by which a Maithil family marks a young life — the birth songs and sixth-day rite of [Sohar & Chhathi](/en/rituals/sohar-chhathi/), the naming, the first feeding of grain (*annaprashan*), and then Mundan. Each step both **celebrates** the child and is felt to **protect** it through the most fragile years.
