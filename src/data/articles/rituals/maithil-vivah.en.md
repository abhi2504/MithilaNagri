A Maithil wedding is unlike most in India, and it is not the affair of a single day. The match is made not by horoscopes but by the **Panji**; the rites run from the matchmaker's first visit through the fourth-day *Chaturthi* and — traditionally — only conclude with the bride's **Dwiragaman** an odd year later. The **stage-by-stage journey above** walks the whole arc from *Ghatkaiti* to *Dwiragaman*. What follows is what makes the Maithil wedding distinct, where it comes from, and why its forms endure.

## A marriage matched by genealogy, not the stars
The single most distinctive thing about a Maithil wedding is how the match is approved. Instead of comparing horoscopes, families turn to a hereditary genealogist, the **panjikar**, who consults the **[Panji](/en/rituals/panji-system/)** registers — instituted in Mithila in the fourteenth century — to certify that bride and groom share no common ancestor within roughly seven paternal and five maternal generations. Only then is the **Siddhant** granted. For centuries these matches were brokered in the open at the **Saurath Sabha**, the great marriage assembly in its mango grove near Madhubani.

## A wedding that once took a year
Because brides and grooms historically married very young, the Maithil rite separated the *wedding* from the *departure*. The ceremonies at the bride's home — Tilak, Matkor, the wedding day, the **Kohbar** nights, and the fourth-day **Chaturthi** that "completes" the marriage — were one event; the bride only left for her husband's house much later, at **Dwiragaman**, in an odd year (the 1st, 3rd, 5th or 7th). The whole marriage could thus span months or years. The rites themselves layer an ancient Vedic core — the *homa* fire, the **Saptapadi** seven steps, *Kanyadan* — over distinctly local customs: the bamboo *marwa*, the **Matkor** soil-rite, the *Aam-Mahu* tree-wedding, the women's *Parichhan* welcome, and the orange-pink *bhuswa* sindur found nowhere else.

## Why the Kohbar is the heart of it
A Maithil marriage is said to be sealed not in the stars but **on the chamber wall**. After the public rites the couple withdraws to the **[Kohbar chamber](/en/rituals/kohbar-ghar/)**, the nuptial room painted with the lotus-pond and bamboo of Madhubani art — a coded prayer for union, fertility and lineage. The playful *Naina-Jogin* recognition game and the most intimate observances all happen here, "begin and end in the Kohbar." It is from this private wedding art that the world later discovered **Madhubani painting**.

## The songs, and today
Through every stage run the **Maithili wedding songs** of the women — by turns teasing the groom, praising the gods, and grieving the bride's *Vidai*. Much has changed: love marriages and dating apps have eclipsed the Saurath assembly; the year-long gap between wedding and Dwiragaman has largely closed; and the whole sequence is often compressed into days. Yet the core — the Panji match, the *marwa*, *Kanyadan* and *Saptapadi*, *Sindurdan*, and above all the Kohbar — survives, carrying a thousand years of Mithila into each new household.
