**Antyeshti**, the "last sacrifice," is the final *saṃskāra* — the rite that returns the body to the elements and sets the soul on its journey to join the ancestors. The **journey above** walks through the cremation, the days of mourning, and the closing rites; here is what they mean and how Mithila keeps them.

## Why the rites are performed
Hindu belief holds that at death the soul has no settled body for its onward passage. The mourners' daily offerings of **pind** — rice-balls with sesame, ghee and water — are understood to build, over the days of mourning, a new subtle body for the departed; the **sapindikaran** rite then formally unites the deceased with the *pitṛs*, the ancestors. The chief mourner (*karta*), usually the eldest son, lights the pyre (*mukhagni*) and a symbolic **Vaitarini** cow-gift is made to help the soul cross the river of the dead. The whole sequence is at once a farewell, a purification of the bereaved, and an act of care that places the dead safely among the forebears.

## The Maithil way
Maithil practice follows the pan-Hindu form but keeps its own handbook and accents: cremation is traditionally not begun until *saava-pahar* (about 3¾ hours after death); the impurity (*asaucha*) lasts **ten nights** for the direct heirs; and — distinctively — the *karta* is restrained from weeping until the closing day, when grief is finally released. Sources differ on whether the cycle culminates on the **twelfth day** (*dwadasha*) or the **thirteenth** (*trayodashi*) with the *shanti* rite and the **Brahma-bhoj** that restores the family to normal life. The donations made on the dead's behalf are received by a specialist funeral priest, the **Mahapatra**.

## The ancestors, renewed each year
The bond does not end with the funeral. The family offers annual **Shraddha** on the death-anniversary and, above all, through the fortnight of **Pitri Paksha**, when *pind* and water (*tarpan*) are given to the whole line of forebears. The supreme site for these rites is nearby **Gaya**, on the Phalgu, where *pind-daan* is believed to grant the ancestors *moksha* — and where, since **1985**, the Brahmins of Mithila have allowed women too to make the offering. So the living renew, generation upon generation, the same lineage so carefully recorded in the [Panji](/en/rituals/panji-system/).
