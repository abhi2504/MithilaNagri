The **Panji Prabandh** is one of the world's oldest continuous systems of recorded genealogy — and, uniquely, one still consulted before a marriage. Maintained on palm-leaf and paper by hereditary registrars, the **panjikars**, it tracks the lineages (*mool*) and clans (*gotra*) of the Maithil Brahmin and Karna Kayastha communities across some six hundred years. The **journey above** walks through how it works, from the king's decree to the Siddhant ruling; here is where it came from and why it still matters.

## When and why it began
By tradition the system was instituted under King **Harisimhadeva** of the Karnat dynasty in the early fourteenth century (commonly **1326 CE**). The trigger, panjikar lore holds, was a marriage discovered to be between distant blood-relatives: to prevent such errors the king ordered the genealogical registration of all Mithila, with the first registrar named as **Gunakar Jha** and the inaugural assembly held at Jamsam near Pandaul. What began as a reform against consanguineous marriage hardened into a vast, living archive of who-descends-from-whom.

## How a panjikar works
A panjikar was no ordinary clerk: historically a candidate trained for about **ten years** at the Darbhanga Raj court and passed an examination before being licensed to certify. Each Panji records the family tree, the earliest known ancestor, the *mool* (ancestral village), the *shakhas* where descendants spread, and the *gotra*. Before a match the registrar checks that bride and groom share no common ancestor within the forbidden degrees — by tradition **seven generations on the father's side and five (some say six) on the mother's** — and only then issues the **Siddhant** (the *asvajanapatra*) that clears the marriage.

## Why it endures — and the threat to it
For centuries these verifications happened in the open at the **[Saurath Sabha](/en/villages/saurath/)**, the marriage assembly in its mango grove near Madhubani, where thousands of families gathered each summer. The Panji is therefore not a dusty register but the spine of Maithil social identity — the reason a community could marry safely across hundreds of dispersed villages. Today, with phone-and-app matchmaking and the decline of the Saurath gathering, the institution is **endangered**: a handful of ageing panjikars hold lakhs of fragile manuscripts, and there is a growing push to digitise and preserve a record-keeping tradition nearly seven centuries old.
