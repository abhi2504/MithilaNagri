Modern Mithila's political story begins with the carving-out of **Bihar**. Announced at the Delhi Durbar of December 1911 and effected on **22 March 1912**, the new province of **Bihar and Orissa** was separated from the Bengal Presidency. Almost immediately, Maithil leaders argued that their Maithili-speaking country was distinct from both Bengal and the Bihari heartland — a case strengthened by **Sir George Grierson's** Linguistic Survey, which had documented Maithili as a language in its own right. The demand for a separate **Mithila** unit thus dates back more than a century, to the very moment Bihar was born.

In the colonial period the cause was carried partly by the Darbhanga Raj. In **1921**, **Maharaja Rameshwar Singh of Darbhanga** submitted a memorial to the Governor seeking recognition of Mithila as a distinct entity. These early efforts produced no new province, but they established Mithila's regional identity as a recurring theme in the politics of north Bihar — one revived after independence and again after the linguistic reorganisation of states in the 1950s, when **Lakshman Jha** gave the demand its modern organisational form from 1952.

The movement's central cultural victory came with the **language**. After decades of campaigning by Maithili writers and organisations, **Maithili was added to the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution** by the **92nd Amendment**: passed by Parliament in December 2003 and brought into force in **January 2004** (along with Bodo, Dogri and Santhali). Recognition gave Maithili official standing in education, examinations and public life, and is widely seen as a turning point in securing the language's future. (Maithili had already won separate Sahitya Akademi recognition in 1966.)

The demand for a separate **Mithila state** itself remains unrealised. It gained fresh energy after the creation of **Jharkhand in 2000** showed that Bihar could be divided, and organisations from cultural bodies to the **Mithila Student Union** have pressed the case on grounds of linguistic identity, cultural distinctiveness and the relative under-development of the Maithili districts. The demand resurfaced prominently in the 2020s, alongside calls for **classical-language status** for Maithili — but no bill to create the state has been introduced, and governments have favoured cultural recognition over redrawing the map.

The making of modern Mithila is therefore a story of identity rather than borders: a region that did not become a province in 1912 or after, but that secured constitutional status for its language in 2003–04 and continues to articulate, through the statehood demand, an enduring sense of itself as a distinct land within the Indian union and across the open border into Nepal's Madhesh.
